## What it is

The B!G Idea runs a creative thinking programme in secondary schools across
Ireland and Northern Ireland. Students work in teams on a project, teachers run
it in class, and mentors from industry give feedback along the way. The
platform is where all of that happens.

The version I inherited was a WordPress site, extended well past what WordPress
is for. I kept it running while I built its replacement, and the new platform
went live in August 2026, eight months after the first commit. In November
2026, The B!G Idea won the Tech 4 Good Service Award at the Technology
Ireland Industry Awards for it.

## My part

I'm the lead developer. I wrote most of the codebase, 686 of the 772 commits
on main, and I own its data model, its service layer and the way it is built
and deployed. When contract developers and an outside design partner joined, I
scoped their work against a shared backlog, wrote the conventions and
onboarding docs they worked from, and reviewed what came in.

## At a glance

- **8 months** from first commit to go-live, January to August 2026
- **5 kinds of user** across 177 screens: students, teachers, mentors, partner companies and staff
- **75 data models** and over 200 migrations
- **About 1,200 automated tests**, run against real PostgreSQL on every pull request
- **About 90 seconds** to deploy, behind an approval gate

## The decisions I'd point at

**Students don't have accounts.** A student is a name on a class roster: no
email, no password. Each team opens its work from a link or a short code that
its teacher can rotate, and student screens show short names. The platform
holds far less personal data about under-18s than a login system would need,
and that one decision reshaped nearly every permission check in the product.

**Safeguarding is part of the flow, not a policy beside it.** Mentor feedback is
moderated before any student sees it, and mentors accept a safeguarding note
before they can give any. Team chat has a filter that catches the usual
workarounds without flagging innocent words. Old support data is pruned on a
schedule, and deleting an account anonymises what it leaves behind in the same
transaction.

**Email is a system, not a pile of templates.** The programme runs to the
school year, so most of its mail is keyed to dates. I built an engine with
typed triggers: events, dates in the programme timeline with an offset,
digests, and manual sends, each with its own conditions and audiences. Repeats
within a window batch into one email. Staff edit the templates themselves and
can switch mail provider at runtime, without a deploy.

**One design system, five audiences.** Teachers, mentors, partner leads,
students and staff each get their own colour, and every screen still comes from
the same component library, with text colour picked for contrast
automatically. A live design system page was the hand-off point for the design
partner, so a fix to one dialog is a fix to all of them.

**Real time where it earns it.** Presence, team chat with typing indicators,
and the live slide position when a teacher presents in class, over Laravel
Reverb.

**Paper surveys, read by the platform.** Plenty of classrooms still do surveys
on paper. Sheets print with registration marks, a photo of a completed sheet is
straightened and each box scored for how filled it is, and anything uncertain
goes to a person instead of being guessed.

**Deny by default.** Staff permissions are set per account, not per job title,
and any admin route without a permission rule is refused. A test fails the
build until the rule exists, so a new page cannot ship open by accident.

**A migration without a big bang.** Existing teachers, mentors and partner
leads came across from WordPress through a dry-run-first import, with schools
matched by roll number and a profile review on first login, so people could fix
what the old system got wrong.

## How it's built and run

Laravel 12 on PHP 8.5, React 19 and TypeScript through Inertia, PostgreSQL,
Redis for queues and cache, and Reverb for WebSockets. Adults sign in without a
password, by magic link or one-time code.

Every pull request runs formatting, linting, type checks, the build and the
full test suite against PostgreSQL. Every bug fix comes with a regression test.
Deploys are started by hand, gated by an approval, built in CI, and take about
a minute and a half. Contractors develop against an anonymised copy of the
database, made by a command that refuses to run against production.
